Section 1

Giving AI what it needs

The honest truth about AI and design work

AI won't automatically make you a better designer. But used well, it can take the parts of design work that are slow, repetitive, or draining — synthesizing 40 interview transcripts, drafting a journey map from scratch, writing the first version of a research readout — and make them significantly faster.

The catch is that "used well" requires knowing what to give it, how to ask, and what to verify before anything goes to a stakeholder. That's what this layer is about.

🧭

AI is a fast, tireless collaborator who will confidently do whatever you ask — whether or not it's right. Your job isn't to prompt it. Your job is to direct it, review it, and own the output.

Why inputs matter more than you think

The quality of what AI produces is almost entirely determined by what you give it. A vague brief gets a vague output. A well-constructed input — real data, clear constraints, a specific ask — gets something you can actually use.

This is what designers mean when they talk about prompting. It's not a magic trick. It's briefing — the same way you'd brief a junior designer or a researcher — except the AI will never push back, never ask a clarifying question, and never tell you it doesn't have enough to go on. It'll just produce something.

So the responsibility for a good brief is entirely yours.

📝

A detailed prompt isn't prescriptive for its own sake. It's a brief. And you already know how to write those.

What works as input

Strong inputs

Interview transcripts

Paste them in full or summarized by question. More raw data = more grounded output.

Video transcripts

Most AI tools can't watch video directly, but exported transcripts work well as text input.

Support ticket logs

Exported CSVs or copied text. The more tickets, the better the pattern recognition — but always ask for evidence (more on this below).

PDFs and PNGs from Mural

Claude and some other tools can read PDFs. For image exports from Mural, describe what's on the board or paste the text content — AI reads images inconsistently.

Existing templates

If your team has a research plan or readout template, paste it in and ask AI to fill it out based on your data. Structured input produces structured output.

Examples of good work

Paste a strong persona or journey map you've made before and say "use this as a format and quality reference."

Weak inputs

"Make me a persona"

No context, no data. AI will invent one that sounds plausible and means nothing.

"Analyze our users"

Who are your users? What data do you have? AI can't answer questions you haven't given it material for.

"Write a journey map for checkout"

For what product? What user? What do you already know? Without context, you'll get a generic output that fits every product and none of them.

The one rule that changes everything

Before you share any AI output with a stakeholder, teammate, or engineer — ask yourself:

Can I point to where this came from?

If the answer is yes — it came from the transcript, the tickets, the template — you're in good shape.

If the answer is no — it came from the AI's general knowledge, or you're not sure — it needs verification before it goes anywhere.

This isn't about distrusting AI. It's about owning your work. When someone asks "where did you get that?" — and they will — you need an answer that isn't "the AI said so."

📸

AI output without evidence is a rough sketch from memory. AI output with evidence is a sketch with a reference photo. Both might look the same — but only one holds up when someone looks closely.

Artifact by artifact

Three artifacts, three scenario cards. Each one shows you what to provide, an example prompt you can copy and adapt, and the specific watch-out for that artifact type.

Scenario card

Personas

What to provide

Personas built from nothing are fiction. Personas built from real data are tools. The difference is what you give the AI to work from.

Strong inputs for personas

  • Interview transcripts
    The more the better. 5+ interviews gives AI enough to identify real patterns across participants.
  • Screener responses
    Who was recruited, what criteria, what they told you before the interview.
  • Existing segments
    If your team already has customer segments or user types defined, include them — AI should build toward known reality, not invent its own.
  • Your persona template
    Paste it in so the output matches your team's format exactly.

Example prompt — copy and adapt

I'm going to paste in transcripts from [X] user interviews. Based only on what appears in these transcripts, I need you to identify 2-3 distinct user types and draft a persona for each.

For each persona:
- Use our template format (pasted below)
- Base every attribute on something a real participant said or did — do not invent details
- If something in the template can't be filled from the transcripts, mark it [NEEDS DATA] rather than making something up
- For each key insight, quote the transcript line that supports it

Here is our persona template:
[paste template]

Here are the transcripts:
[paste transcripts]

Watch out for — the invented detail problem

AI will fill in persona details that sound real but aren't grounded in your data. A persona that says "Maya checks her phone 47 times a day and prefers asynchronous communication" didn't come from your interviews — it came from general knowledge about user behavior that matched the pattern.

The [NEEDS DATA] instruction is doing important work. It forces AI to flag gaps rather than fill them with plausible fiction. Review every attribute and ask: did someone actually say this?

Scenario card

User Journey Maps

What to provide

Journey maps are only as good as the reality they reflect. AI can help you structure, synthesize, and identify gaps — but it needs your data to do it honestly.

Strong inputs for journey maps

  • Interview transcripts
    Especially moments where participants described a process step by step.
  • Existing journey maps
    Even rough ones. AI is excellent at improving and pressure-testing a draft you've already started.
  • Support ticket logs
    Tickets show where the journey breaks — high-volume ticket categories map directly to pain point moments.
  • Service context
    What are the touchpoints? What channels exist? What systems are involved? AI can't know your product's specifics without being told.

Example prompt — copy and adapt

I need to build a user journey map for [user type] completing [task or process].

I'm going to give you:
1. Transcripts from interviews where participants described this process
2. Support ticket themes from this part of the experience
3. The stages we use in our journey map template

Based only on this material, please:
- Map the journey across our stages
- Identify emotions and pain points at each stage, quoting specific transcript moments where possible
- Flag any stages where you don't have enough data to make a grounded observation — mark these [NEEDS VALIDATION]
- Identify gaps: are there stages we should have data on but don't?

Our journey map stages are:
[paste your stages]

Transcripts:
[paste]

Support ticket themes:
[paste]

Watch out for — the gap-filling problem

Journey maps have stages. Stages need content. AI will fill every stage whether or not you have data for it — because an empty stage looks wrong to a pattern-matching system trained on complete documents.

The [NEEDS VALIDATION] instruction forces honesty about where the map is grounded and where it's speculative. A journey map with some [NEEDS VALIDATION] flags is more useful than a complete one built on invented details — it tells you exactly where your next research effort should go.

Bonus — pressure-testing an existing map

This is one of the highest-value uses of AI in design work — not generating from scratch, but reviewing what you already have.

Here's a journey map our team built:
[paste map]

Review it and tell me:
1. What stages feel underspecified or vague?
2. Where are we likely missing touchpoints or emotions?
3. What questions would a skeptical stakeholder ask about this map that we should be able to answer?

Scenario card

Research Readouts

What to provide

The goal of a research readout is to present findings stakeholders can act on. AI can help you synthesize and structure — but every finding needs to trace back to your actual data. If it can't, it shouldn't be in the readout.

Strong inputs for research readouts

  • Your research data in full
    Transcripts, tickets, survey results — paste the actual source material, not a summary of it. AI synthesizes better from raw data than from your interpretation of raw data.
  • Your readout template
    If your team has one, paste it. AI should fill your format, not invent its own.
  • Your research questions
    What were you trying to learn? AI should organize findings around your actual questions, not whatever patterns it finds most interesting.
  • Any known context
    What does the team already believe? What hypotheses were you testing? This helps AI distinguish between confirming findings and surprising ones.

Example prompt — copy and adapt

I need to write a research readout based on the data I'm going to paste below.

Ground rules:
- Base every finding only on the data I provide — do not add outside knowledge, invented statistics, or general best practices
- For every finding, include at least two direct quotes or specific examples from the source data that support it
- If you identify a pattern but can only find one example supporting it, flag it as [SINGLE INSTANCE — NEEDS VALIDATION] rather than presenting it as a finding
- Do not include any percentages, averages, or statistics unless they appear explicitly in my data
- Use our readout template structure

Our research questions were:
[paste questions]

Our readout template:
[paste template]

Source data:
[paste transcripts / tickets / survey results]

Watch out for — the confident statistic problem

AI will generate statistics that sound precise and credible — "2.5 handoffs on average," "support time doubles," "67% of users reported" — even when your data contains nothing of the sort.

It learned from millions of documents where that kind of statement appears. It knows what a research finding is supposed to look like. So it produces one.

The instruction "do not include any percentages, averages, or statistics unless they appear explicitly in my data" is not optional. It's the difference between a readout you can defend and one that falls apart the moment someone asks for the source.

📊

A statistic without a source is decoration, not evidence. It makes the readout look more rigorous while actually making it less trustworthy. If you can't point to where a number came from, cut it.

Before this goes to a stakeholder

  • Every finding has at least two supporting examples from the actual source data
  • Every statistic has a real source — either your data explicitly or a cited external study
  • [NEEDS VALIDATION] flags have been either validated or removed
  • You can answer "where did you get that?" for every claim on the page
  • Findings have been reviewed by at least one other person familiar with the research

Want to use this as a real checklist? Copy it into Notion or your team's research template.

Section 3

Trust and verification

Why AI makes things up — and why it sounds so confident

Understanding why hallucination happens makes it less scary and more manageable.

AI models learned by reading enormous amounts of text. In that text, certain patterns appeared constantly — research findings have statistics, personas have specific details, journey maps have emotions at every stage. The model learned what these things are supposed to look like.

When you ask for a research readout, it produces something that matches the pattern of a research readout — including the statistics, the specific details, the confident language. It's not fabricating intentionally. It doesn't have intentions. It's pattern-matching to what a research readout looks like, and sometimes the pattern includes specifics that weren't in your data.

🎭

AI learned to sound like an expert by reading experts. That's different from being one. Sounding confident and being accurate are not the same thing — and AI cannot always tell the difference.

Three categories of output and how to treat them

✅High trust

Structure and format

Examples: Document structure, section headers, template formatting, rewriting something you wrote in a cleaner way

Review needed: Light — check that it matches your intent

⚠️Medium trust

Synthesis from your data

Examples: Themes from transcripts you provided, patterns in tickets you pasted, findings from research you gave it

Review needed: Moderate — verify findings against source, check for invented specifics, confirm quotes are accurate

🛑Low trust

Knowledge from training

Examples: Statistics it generates, industry benchmarks, best practices, anything it "knows" rather than learned from your data

Review needed: Heavy — verify any statistic independently, treat general claims as hypotheses not facts, always find the source before sharing
